Access Architecture

The study and design of conditions that let preserved ability become usable action.

People can possess an ability without being able to access it reliably when it is needed. Access Architecture examines the conditions that make ability available, unstable, fragile, or difficult to recover.

Read the foundational paperStart with plain language

Ability is not access.

Ability describes what a person is capable of doing. Access describes whether the conditions currently allow that capacity to become usable action.

A person may reason clearly, explain a solution, demonstrate expertise, or perform at a high level in one context while remaining unable to reliably initiate, sustain, switch, resume, organize, or complete action in another.

Visible competence is real. It is not proof that equivalent access is stable, available on demand, or sustainable over time.

The field examines

Conditions, not just capacities.

Access is shaped by interacting conditions. The relevant barrier may change over time, and a support that works once may not remain a fit.

Environment

Noise, layout, tools, pacing, interruption, and the physical conditions around action.

Body and mind

Fatigue, pain, sleep, workload, attention, initiation, recovery, and physiological state.

Relationships

Communication, trust, co-regulation, support, credibility, and shared responsibility.

Time

Fluctuation, episodic disability, changing needs, task resumption, and reliability.

Systems

Workplaces, institutions, accommodations, policies, and their assumptions.

Design

Conditions that preserve context, reduce access burden, and help ability become action.

Working vocabulary

A language for what changes.

These concepts are part of an evolving framework. They name patterns often experienced but poorly described by static models of ability and accommodation.

Recognition Paradox

Visible competence can reduce the perceived need for support.

Explore the concept →

Access Volatility

The conditions that make capacity usable change over time.

Accommodation Drift

A once-effective support loses fit as needs or conditions change.

Access Fragility

A hard-won accessible state is comparatively easy to disrupt.

Context Collapse

Enough task context is lost that meaningful reconstruction is required.

Re-entry Failure

Previously accessible work cannot be reliably resumed after disruption.

Where the field crosses

A meeting place for disciplines.

Access Architecture is not a replacement for existing fields. It brings several bodies of knowledge into conversation around a shared question: what makes preserved ability usable here, now, under these conditions?

Disability studies

Social, cultural, political, and lived-experience accounts of disability and access.

Accessibility and human factors

Designing environments, tools, and systems around real human variation.

Cognitive science

Attention, workload, memory, initiation, interruption, and task resumption.

Neurodiversity

Variation in cognition, regulation, communication, and the conditions that support participation.

Systems thinking

Interactions between people, environments, institutions, policies, and feedback loops.

Lived experience and relational inquiry

Knowledge developed through situated experience, collaboration, and shared meaning-making.

Established infrastructure

Resources.

Access Architecture sits alongside established accessibility law, standards, and guidance. These resources define important obligations and design criteria; they do not, by themselves, describe every condition that makes access reliable from moment to moment.

ADA Standards

The U.S. Department of Justice’s standards establish requirements for physical accessibility in covered buildings and facilities.

Scope boundary: compliance with a physical-access standard does not establish that a person can reliably use a service, task, or environment under changing conditions.

Episodic disability & the ADA

The ADA, as amended, recognizes that an impairment can qualify when it would substantially limit a major life activity while active, even if it is episodic or in remission.

Read the EEOC guidance →
Read Canada’s Accessible Canada Act summary →

Scope boundary: legal recognition supports protection and accommodation; it does not by itself describe the conditions that make access fluctuate in daily life.

Episodic Disabilities Network

A Canadian network focused on policy, employment, income support, and removing barriers created by the episodic nature of disability.

Relation to AA: it makes the changing and sometimes unpredictable character of disability visible in policy and workplace conversations.

Scope boundary: this is a network and resource hub, not a legal standard or a replacement for individualized advice.

Section 508

Federal information and communication technology requirements make covered electronic content and systems accessible and usable.

Scope boundary: technical conformance is essential, but it cannot alone measure interruption, recovery, cognitive load, or access volatility.

WCAG 2.2

W3C’s Web Content Accessibility Guidelines provide testable recommendations for making web content more perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust.

Scope boundary: WCAG evaluates digital content and interfaces; Access Architecture also asks whether the person can sustain access across context and time.

WAI guidance

The Web Accessibility Initiative gathers guidance, techniques, and educational resources for accessible web design and development.

Relation to AA: this is practical accessibility infrastructure that helps turn inclusive intent into concrete design decisions.

How the pieces fit: established standards can make access possible and measurable. Access Architecture studies the conditions that determine whether that access remains usable, recoverable, and reliable in lived experience.

Common questions

A short orientation.

These answers describe the field as it currently stands. The framework is developing, and its concepts remain open to refinement.

What is Access Architecture?

Access Architecture is the study and design of conditions that let preserved ability become usable action. It asks why a person may have an ability but not reliable access to it under particular conditions.

What does “ability is not access” mean?

It means that being capable of something in one context does not prove that the same capacity will be available on demand, repeatedly, or sustainably in another context.

Is Access Architecture a diagnosis or treatment model?

No. It is an emerging conceptual and design field. It does not replace clinical assessment, diagnosis, disability determination, or individualized professional support.

How is this different from accessibility?

Accessibility is a crucial foundation. Access Architecture focuses more specifically on the changing conditions that influence whether a preserved capacity can become usable action, including volatility, interruption, recovery, and accommodation fit.

Who is this field for?

It is relevant to people navigating fluctuating access, researchers, designers, educators, clinicians, employers, institutions, and anyone trying to understand the gap between capacity and reliable participation.
